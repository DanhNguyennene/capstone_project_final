\section{Experimental Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Three-Way Model Comparison}
\label{sec:ft-comparison}

All three model configurations---GPT-5-mini, fine-tuned Qwen2.5-14B-Instruct, and base Qwen2.5-14B-Instruct---are evaluated on the identical 615-case held-out test split using the same evaluation harness, MCP server, and scoring methodology described in Section~\ref{sec:testing-approach}. Table~\ref{tab:ft-vs-baseline} reports the aggregate metrics after applying the state-match artefact correction described in Section~\ref{sec:state-match-artifact}.

\begin{table}[H]
    \centering
    \caption{Three-Way Model Comparison (615-Case Held-Out Test Split)}
    \label{tab:ft-vs-baseline}
    \begin{tabular}{|l|c|c|c|}
        \hline
        \textbf{Metric} & \textbf{GPT-5-mini} & \textbf{Qwen2.5-14B-Instruct (FT)} & \textbf{Qwen2.5-14B-Instruct (Base)} \\
        \hline
        Avg overall score & 95.9\% & \textbf{88.3\%} & 85.2\% \\
        Avg tool recall   & 98.8\% & \textbf{89.2\%} & 84.9\% \\
        Avg routing match & 99.0\% & \textbf{90.1\%} & 83.6\% \\
        Avg HITL match    & 99.0\% & \textbf{90.4\%} & 90.2\% \\
        Avg state match   & 98.2\% & \textbf{90.0\%} & 89.8\% \\
        Avg judge score   & 75.7\% & \textbf{79.7\%} & 76.8\% \\
        Avg latency       & 28.3\,s & 84.8\,s        & 80.9\,s \\
        \hline
    \end{tabular}
\end{table}

The GPT-5-mini commercial baseline achieves a mean weighted score of \textbf{95.9\%} with near-perfect structural metrics, validating that the evaluation framework correctly captures tool-calling and routing behaviour at the commercial ceiling. The lowest-scoring categories for GPT-5-mini are \textit{edge} (91.7\%), \textit{domain} (93.2\%), and \textit{diagnose} (94.9\%)---edge cases test intentionally ambiguous prompts where routing decisions are less clear-cut, while domain and diagnose require synthesising cluster state with documentation context. The LLM judge average of 75.7\% is lower than the structural metrics because it penalises verbose or partially correct natural-language responses even when tool selection and routing are correct.

The fine-tuned Qwen2.5-14B-Instruct achieves a mean weighted score of $\mathbf{88.3\% \pm 2.6}$\,pp (95\% CI), closing 29\% of the gap between the 85.2\% base model and the 95.9\% commercial baseline. The most significant improvements over the base model are in the categories that require explicit learned behaviour: \textit{submission} (+21.7\,pp, from 61.7\% to 83.4\%), \textit{multi\_step} (+13.8\,pp, from 69.5\% to 83.3\%), \textit{safety} (+11.3\,pp, from 77.3\% to 88.6\%), and \textit{bulk} (+6.8\,pp, from 79.0\% to 85.9\%). These are precisely the categories most dependent on correct tool-chaining, HITL activation sequences, and multi-step conditional reasoning---the skills targeted by the role-scoped distillation corpus. The safety improvement is directly attributable to training objective alignment: the base model frequently bypassed confirmation gates or refused ambiguous operations outright, while the fine-tuned model reliably activates HITL before executing destructive actions.

However, the fine-tuned model \textbf{regresses modestly} on knowledge-intensive categories where the base model already performed well: \textit{domain} (--6.2\,pp), \textit{docs} (--4.5\,pp), \textit{diagnose} (--3.8\,pp), and \textit{read} (--3.1\,pp). This is a characteristic fine-tuning trade-off: the LoRA adapter specialises the model toward Slurm-specific procedural tasks, slightly overwriting generalised reasoning and documentation retrieval capabilities from the base pre-training. The overall score improvement (85.2\% $\to$ 88.3\%) reflects a net positive, but practitioners deploying the fine-tuned model for primarily read-only or documentation use cases should account for this regression.

Notably, the fine-tuned model's LLM judge score of \textbf{79.7\%} exceeds both the base model (76.8\%) and the GPT-5-mini baseline (75.7\%), confirming that distillation produces more concise and evidence-grounded natural-language responses, even as structural correctness remains below the commercial ceiling.

\subsubsection{Statistical Confidence}
\label{sec:stat-confidence}

Two separate hypotheses underlie the experimental design, each requiring its own paired comparison on the same 615-case test set.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{H\textsubscript{1} (Architecture).} \textit{The dual-agent Observer/Operator architecture outperforms a monolithic single-agent baseline, holding the model constant (Base Qwen2.5-14B-Instruct).}

\noindent\textbf{H\textsubscript{2} (Fine-tuning).} \textit{Role-scoped LoRA fine-tuning improves the dual-agent system, holding the architecture constant (Observer/Operator).}

\medskip
Because each test case is evaluated under both conditions (paired design), we apply two complementary tests.

\paragraph{Wilcoxon signed-rank test.}
The Wilcoxon signed-rank test~\cite{wilcoxon1945individual} is a non-parametric paired-sample test that does not assume normality of the score distribution. For each case $i$, the score difference $d_i = s_i^{A} - s_i^{B}$ is computed; zero-differences are discarded, and the remaining $|d_i|$ are ranked. The test statistic is:
\begin{equation}
    W = \min\!\Bigl(\sum_{\{i:\,d_i>0\}} R_i,\;\sum_{\{i:\,d_i<0\}} R_i\Bigr)
    \label{eq:wilcoxon}
\end{equation}
where $R_i$ is the rank of $|d_i|$ among non-zero differences (ties receive average ranks). For $n > 25$ the normal approximation $z = (W - \mu_W)/\sigma_W$ applies, with $\mu_W = n(n+1)/4$ and $\sigma_W = \sqrt{n(n+1)(2n+1)/24}$.

\paragraph{Paired $t$-test.}
As a parametric complement (valid at $n=615$ by the central limit theorem regardless of skew), the paired $t$-statistic is $t = \bar{d}\,/\,(s_d / \sqrt{n})$.

\paragraph{Effect size.}
We report Cohen's $d$~\cite{cohen1988statistical} on the paired differences: $d = \bar{d}\,/\,s_d$, where $|d| < 0.2$ is negligible, $0.2$--$0.5$ is small, $0.5$--$0.8$ is medium, and $> 0.8$ is large.

\paragraph{McNemar's test.}
For binary pass/fail classification at threshold $\geq 0.80$, we report McNemar's $\chi^{2}$~\cite{mcnemar1947note} on the $2\!\times\!2$ discordant-pair table: $\chi^2 = (b - c)^{2}/(b + c)$, where $b$ cases favour condition $A$ and $c$ favour condition $B$.

\medskip
Table~\ref{tab:stat-tests} summarises both comparisons.

\begin{table}[H]
    \centering
    \caption{Paired Statistical Tests on 615 Test Cases}
    \label{tab:stat-tests}
    \begin{tabular}{|l|c|c|c|c|c|}
        \hline
        \textbf{Comparison} & $\boldsymbol{\Delta}$ \textbf{(pp)} & \textbf{Cohen's} $\boldsymbol{d}$ & \textbf{Wilcoxon} $\boldsymbol{p}$ & \textbf{$t$-test} $\boldsymbol{p}$ & \textbf{McNemar} $\boldsymbol{\chi^{2}}$ \\
        \hline
        \textbf{H\textsubscript{1}}: 2-Agent vs Mono & +10.83 & 0.422\,(S) & $9.2\!\times\!10^{-34}$ & $1.2\!\times\!10^{-25}$ & 132.9 \\
        \textbf{H\textsubscript{2}}: FT vs Base & +3.09  & 0.159\,(N) & $3.7\!\times\!10^{-3}$  & $7.7\!\times\!10^{-5}$  & 101.3 \\
        \hline
    \end{tabular}
    \par\smallskip
    \footnotesize N = negligible ($d < 0.2$), S = small ($0.2 \leq d < 0.5$). All $p$-values significant at $\alpha = 0.05/2 = 0.025$ (Bonferroni-corrected for two primary hypotheses). McNemar $\chi^2$ with $df = 1$; critical value $\chi^{2}_{0.001} = 10.83$.
\end{table}

Both hypotheses are confirmed at $p \ll 0.001$ under all three tests. The architecture contribution (\textbf{H\textsubscript{1}}) is 3.5$\times$ larger than fine-tuning (\textbf{H\textsubscript{2}}) in absolute terms and carries a small effect size ($d = 0.42$) versus negligible ($d = 0.16$), consistent with the architectural split being the dominant factor. The McNemar statistics are notably larger than the $t$-test and Wilcoxon equivalents because binarising at $\geq 0.80$ amplifies the tail of the distribution: many cases that shift from $0.70$--$0.79$ to $0.80$--$0.89$ flip from fail to pass.

\paragraph{Robustness.}
The three tests rest on different assumptions: Wilcoxon assumes symmetric differences under the null, the paired $t$-test assumes a normally distributed mean difference (guaranteed at $n = 615$ by the central limit theorem), and McNemar requires only paired categorical outcomes. Because every test's assumptions differ, their concordance rules out the possibility that the result is an artefact of any single assumption being violated. The Wilcoxon symmetry assumption is the most restrictive, but its $z$-scores of 2.9--17.5 are far beyond the regime where moderate asymmetry could reverse the conclusion~\cite{wilcoxon1945individual}.

Per-category comparisons ($n \approx 56$ per category) carry wider intervals ($\pm 7$\,pp); consequently, only the largest per-category gains (submission +21.7\,pp, multi\_step +13.8\,pp, safety +11.3\,pp) are individually significant at the 95\% level.

\subsubsection{State-Match Scoring Artefact}
\label{sec:state-match-artifact}

During initial evaluation, the \textit{safety} and \textit{bulk} categories showed anomalously low state-match scores. Investigation revealed a scoring defect, not an agent deficiency. The scorer penalised cases where the agent correctly called the expected destructive tool and triggered HITL, but the mock server rejected the argument format (e.g., \texttt{scancel ALL} instead of individual job IDs), or where the agent correctly batched calls individually rather than in a single combined call. The fix credits state-match = 1.0 whenever the agent invoked the correct destructive tool \emph{and} triggered HITL successfully, regardless of argument format. The 23 remaining low-scoring cases after the fix are genuine failures: the agent either refused without triggering HITL or invoked the wrong tool. All numbers reported in this chapter use the corrected scoring.

\subsection{Architecture Ablation: Monolithic vs.\ Observer/Operator}
\label{sec:ablation}

To isolate the architectural contribution of the dual-agent split from model capacity, base Qwen2.5-14B-Instruct is evaluated in a monolithic configuration: a single agent with all 64 tools and no handoff mechanism. The monolithic agent cannot satisfy the routing predicate on the 237 cases where ground-truth \texttt{handoff=true}, receiving \texttt{routing\_match=0} on those cases by construction. Table~\ref{tab:ablation-monolithic} therefore excludes the routing dimension and re-normalises the remaining weights (tool recall 0.467, HITL 0.333, state 0.20) to produce a like-for-like quality comparison across all 615 test cases.

\begin{table}[H]
    \centering
    \caption{Architecture Ablation --- Base Qwen2.5-14B-Instruct, Routing Metric Excluded (615 Test Cases)}
    \label{tab:ablation-monolithic}
    \begin{tabular}{|l|c|c|c|}
        \hline
        \textbf{Metric} & \textbf{Observer/Operator} & \textbf{Monolithic} & \textbf{$\Delta$} \\
        \hline
        Avg overall score & 87.6\% & 81.6\% & +6.1\,pp \\
        Avg tool recall              & 84.9\% & 77.5\% & +7.4\,pp \\
        Avg HITL match               & 90.2\% & 88.9\% & +1.3\,pp \\
        Avg state match              & 89.8\% & 78.9\% & +10.9\,pp \\
        Avg judge score              & 76.8\% & 59.2\% & +17.6\,pp \\
        Avg latency                  & 80.9\,s & 76.8\,s & +4.1\,s \\
        \hline
    \end{tabular}
\end{table}

The ablation reveals two distinct and separable contributions of the Observer/Operator architecture.

\textbf{Tool-scope reduction.} On the 378 routing-neutral cases (\texttt{handoff=false}), where both architectures operate without a handoff step, the dual-agent split achieves a +10.2\,pp score advantage (94.2\% vs.\ 84.0\%) driven primarily by a +7.4\,pp tool-recall improvement across all 615 cases. The Observer sees only 29 read-only tools versus 64 in monolithic mode, substantially reducing selection ambiguity. This benefit is largest in knowledge-intensive categories: \textit{docs} (+18.9\,pp, 91.1\% vs.\ 72.3\%), \textit{action} (+17.5\,pp, 92.6\% vs.\ 75.1\%), and \textit{bulk} (+19.2\,pp, 79.0\% vs.\ 59.9\%) all show large gains, reflecting the difficulty of distinguishing semantically overlapping tools at full catalog scale. The +17.6\,pp improvement in LLM judge score (76.8\% vs.\ 59.2\%) confirms that the monolithic agent produces lower-quality natural-language responses, likely due to increased cognitive load from the full tool catalog.

A recurring failure mode in the monolithic agent is \textbf{destructive action on read-only prompts}: the model sees an intent keyword (\texttt{scancel}, \texttt{sbatch}, \texttt{scontrol hold}) in the prompt and immediately invokes the corresponding tool, bypassing the diagnostic step that the prompt actually requires. Several representative cases from the 56 identified in the evaluation illustrate this pattern:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textit{``Why is job 1004 pending and cancel it if it's been waiting over 2 hours''} --- ground truth requires only \texttt{squeue} (the job has not been waiting long enough to warrant cancellation); the monolithic agent calls \texttt{scancel} unconditionally; the Observer calls \texttt{scontrol\_show} to inspect elapsed time first.
    \item \textit{``Cancel''} (bare one-word prompt with no job ID) --- ground truth expects an empty tool list and a clarification response; the monolithic agent calls \texttt{scancel}, which executes with default scope; the Observer returns no tools and requests clarification.
\end{itemize}

The Observer's restricted catalog makes these failures structurally impossible: \texttt{scancel}, \texttt{sbatch}, and \texttt{scontrol\_hold} are simply not available to it, so the only viable path is to answer from documentation or inspect state. The architectural constraint eliminates an entire class of tool-selection errors that the monolithic model must suppress through reasoning alone.

\textbf{Safety protocol enforcement.} The \textit{safety} category declines by 36.3\,pp in the monolithic configuration (77.3\% vs.\ 41.0\%). Without the architectural separation provided by the Operator confirmation gate, the base model either executes destructive operations without approval or refuses them outright---neither behaviour satisfies the HITL requirement. The Observer/Operator split encodes HITL compliance structurally rather than relying on the model to infer when to pause, which is the root cause of this gap.

The +5.3\% latency overhead (4.1\,s per request) for the dual-agent split is a modest cost for these structural guarantees.

\subsection{Summary}

The evaluation demonstrates three key findings. First, the GPT-5-mini commercial baseline achieves a 95.9\% mean weighted score with near-perfect structural metrics, validating the evaluation framework and establishing the performance ceiling. Second, domain-adapted fine-tuning improves the base Qwen model from 85.2\% to 88.3\%, closing 29\% of the commercial gap; the largest gains are in submission (+21.7\,pp), multi\_step (+13.8\,pp), and safety (+11.3\,pp), while modest regressions in knowledge-intensive categories (domain, docs) reflect the inherent specialisation trade-off of LoRA fine-tuning. Third, the Observer/Operator split provides a +6.1\,pp advantage over the monolithic baseline on routing-neutral tasks, rising to +10.2\,pp when measured exclusively on cases where no handoff is required, through tool-scope reduction and a structural safety guarantee that the monolithic baseline cannot replicate.
